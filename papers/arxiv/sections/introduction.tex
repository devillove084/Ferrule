\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Storage-backed weight residency lets an inference owner repeatedly
materialize selected weights into a bounded execution cache. The immediate
problem is to bound those copies and reuse their capacity safely, whether or
not the complete compact checkpoint could fit in host memory. Exceeding a
device's memory, exceeding aggregate physical residency, and choosing a
controlled cache budget are different conditions. A checkpoint's file size,
an expanded tensor's size, and a request's working set are not interchangeable.
The working set also includes activations, model state, staging, outstanding
transfers, allocator/driver reserves, metadata, and resident file-backed pages.

\system{} studies bounded storage-backed residency through
\emph{plan, admit, materialize, publish, execute, retire}. A source-bound plan
fixes what is read; admission bounds covered claims; completion validates the
requested generation; custody prevents premature reuse. Its concrete
next-expert pipeline separates the read window from owner compute submission.
A safe blocking control waits on the same worker immediately, whereas
lookahead submits the next selected read after current upload and before
current compute submission. The whole forward remains synchronous. These
contracts make scheduling and resource reuse testable; they do not themselves
prove storage necessity, hardware overlap, model quality, or a speedup.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
We organize the paper around four questions, each with a concrete observable:
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 -- capacity.] Can a complete, quality-accepted request finish
  with bounded repeated materialization while the measured working set stays
  within declared per-domain limits? A successful configured-budget run is
  not, by itself, evidence that the model exceeds physical memory.
  \item[RQ2 -- request I/O.] What request-time demand is visible at the
  logical-reader and NFS-client levels, and what attribution or backing-store
  traffic remains unknown after separating startup and cache effects?
  \item[RQ3 -- safe scheduling.] Under the same representation, quality
  contract, and budgets, what differs between the implemented safe blocking
  and bounded lookahead schedules in demand waiting, speculative work, and
  correlated read/transfer/kernel activity?
  \item[RQ4 -- custody and failure.] Do exact identities, credits, and
  custody prevent stale publication and premature reuse, while retaining
  capacity when completion is Unknown and releasing it only after evidence of
  quiescence?
\end{description}

\paragraph{Contributions and boundaries.}
This manuscript makes four scoped contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a resource model that separates disjoint physical domains, configured
  accounting domains, representations, and the measured time-varying working
  set;
  \item a normal-path contract that connects source-bound planning and
  stage-local admission to generation-qualified completion, publication, and
  retirement;
  \item structured success/terminal-custody algorithms and an implemented
  bounded next-expert schedule with an identical-budget blocking control; and
  \item an evidence-oriented evaluation layout that ties each RQ to tables,
  traces, memory peaks, and plots rather than promoting design intent to a
  result.
\end{enumerate}

The historical serving evidence is deliberately narrow. TP1 attempt
\texttt{-02} is one DEBUG-instrumented exploratory run ($n=1$): four HTTP
requests completed, the three limited visible-text/eight-output-token-usage
canaries passed, and Hello2 passed HTTP and usage checks only. It records
30 decoder invocations and 36,240,064,128 logical checkpoint-reader bytes;
these are logical bytes, not physical NAS/network or block-device reads.
Generated IDs, full numerical quality, process-owned GPU attribution, and a
complete resource contract are absent or unknown. Attempt \texttt{-01} failed
before server launch and supplied no request or read result. Consequently,
TP1 does not establish RQ1 capacity, storage necessity, RQ3 scheduling, or
RQ4 custody closure.

Separate TP2 receipts include numerical/lifecycle observations for a test
binary and descriptive resident comparisons. They are not interchangeable
with TP1: the TP2 test binary is not a public serving binary, the arithmetic
and topology contracts are profile-specific, and the two resident comparison
blocks are not a fair speedup proof. In particular, the vLLM supplement is
not evidence of storage necessity or a matched total working-set budget. Both opt-in schedules now have same-cut real-GPU factory qualification:
nine exact-once gates pass on \texttt{033519c}. This closes their selected
numerical/provider gate, not CLI/HTTP, a capacity frontier, paired performance,
or physical release. Historical failures and wrapper outcomes remain separate
(Appendix~\ref{sec:historical-chronology}); no old PASS is borrowed. Multimodal, unified-memory, universal-registry, ACID, and default
NCCL/device-collective claims are outside the demonstrated evidence. Storage
inference and prefetch are prior art; no first-system or state-of-the-art
performance claim is made.

The rest of the paper follows the execution argument. Section~\ref{sec:scope}
defines representations, physical and accounting domains, quality, and
failure contracts. Sections~\ref{sec:normal-profile}, \ref{sec:invariants},
and~\ref{sec:algorithms} follow a normal profile, state its invariants, and
give structured algorithms. Section~\ref{sec:implementation}
identifies the code paths and their limits. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}
starts with the frozen setup and then reports RQ1--RQ4 in order. Sections~\ref{sec:related}, \ref{sec:limitations}, and~\ref{sec:conclusion}
place the design in prior work, collect the remaining evidence gates, and
conclude.
